The Cross-Ratio Ruler

A picture squeezes distances, and squeezes the far ones most; but four points on a line keep one number that no picture can change, their cross-ratio. Calibrate a ruler on three known marks and it reads true distance straight off the picture, wherever the painter stood.

Plate I. A straight road, a sign at ten metres, and a ruler graduated by cross-ratio.

a.Three brass pins set the ruler: one on the near mark (0 m), one on the sign (10 m), and one on the horizon, which is the picture of the point at infinity. Drag them; they catch when they come near their marks.

b.The graduation is cut from those three pins alone: a height y is marked t = 10·(y−y₀)(y₁₀−y∞)/((y₁₀−y₀)(y−y∞)). The divisions crowd together toward the horizon, as the road does.

c.The red staff stands on the road. Drag it near or far; the ruler reads its foot.

d.The posts stand every ten metres. Count them against the ruler: they fall on 10, 20, 30, as they must.

e.Turn the dials and the picture changes. Untick the pins follow first, and you will see the ruler fall off its marks and read wrongly until it is laid again.

Plate II. A floor of square tiles. One tile is pinned at its four corners; the rest follow.

a.Four pins, at the corners of one tile, are the whole calibration. Drag any of them: the floor redraws as the picture of the same square grid from wherever a painter could have stood.

b.The horizon is where the floor's lines meet: the picture of the line at infinity. Each family of parallel joints runs to its own vanishing point on it.

c.Drag the lamp about the floor. Its place is read in tiles, through the one transformation that carries the pinned tile back to a true square.

d.On a line the ruler needs three marks; on a plane it needs four. A projective map of the line has three degrees of freedom, and of the plane, eight: four points, two numbers each.

Plate III. With a straightedge only, find the fourth point D that makes (A, B; C, D) = −1.

a.Mark A, B, C on the line. Each can be dragged along it.

b.Choose any point L off the line, and any point M on the ray from L to C. Draw the lines from L to A and B, and from M to A and B. They cross at K and N.

c.The line through K and N meets the first line at D. Now drag L and M wherever you like: the figure swings wildly, and D does not move.

d.Everything here is lines through points, so a picture of the figure is the same construction. That is why D cannot depend on L and M: the cross-ratio −1 is fixed by A, B, C alone.

e.Slide C to the middle of AB and D runs off to infinity. Painters use this in reverse: the diagonals of a tile in perspective find its middle.

¶Commentary

Perspective is projective. A painter standing at distance d from the near mark, with the eye at height h, sends the point t metres down the road to the height y = y∞ + (y₀ − y∞)·d/(t + d) in the picture. Maps of the form y = (at + b)/(ct + d) are the projective transformations of a line, the group PGL(2, ℝ). They change lengths, and they change ratios of lengths.

The one invariant. For four points on a line, the cross-ratio (A, B; C, D) = (AC·BD)/(BC·AD), with signed lengths, is unchanged by every projective map. It does for PGL(2) what length does for sliding and angle does for turning, and it is the only such number: any quantity that perspective preserves is a function of it.

The ruler. Take the near mark, the sign, and the horizon, at 0, 10, and ∞ metres. For a fourth height y, the cross-ratio in the picture equals the cross-ratio on the road, and solving gives the distance. The scale the ruler carries is just this formula, cut into wood. A ruler laid on proportionally, as if the picture were drawn to scale, is right at the sign and wrong everywhere else, without bound toward the horizon.

On a plane. A projective map of the plane is fixed by where it sends four points in general position, so one tile, pinned at its corners, calibrates the whole floor. The map that carries the pinned tile back to a unit square carries every other point to its true place in tiles. Along any line of the floor the old one-dimensional ruler still works.

Harmonic sets. When the cross-ratio is −1 the four points are called harmonic, and D is the harmonic conjugate of C with respect to A and B. The complete quadrilateral of Plate III constructs it with a straightedge alone. The proof is a single sentence: project the figure so that the line KN goes to infinity, and the construction becomes a parallelogram, whose diagonals bisect each other.

Further. Albrecht Dürer's and Piero della Francesca's treatises on perspective; Desargues's Brouillon project (1639), where the harmonic construction first appears in this form; and the opening chapters of Coxeter's Projective Geometry.
