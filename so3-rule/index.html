The Rule of Turns

A slide rule for SO(3), the group of rotations of space, after Hamilton.

Every rotation of a sphere is a turn about some axis through some angle. Draw it as an arc of a great circle, half as long as the angle, on the circle whose pole is the axis, and let the arc slide freely along its circle. Then two rotations compose like two sides of a spherical triangle: slide the arcs until they meet head to tail, and the third side is the product, read off the globe as a slide rule is read.

Plate I. The globe, two slides, and the cursor that closes the triangle.

a.The globe is ruled every 15° of latitude and longitude, with N at the top. Drag anywhere on the plate that is not brass to turn it and look round the back.

b.Each slide is a turn: an arc of length θ/2 on the great circle whose pole is the axis. It is cut every 5° of arc and figured in the full angle θ, so the figure at the arrow is the rotation. Drag a slide along its circle: the rotation it stands for does not change.

c.The poles a and b are the axes. Drag one to tip the axis; the slide stays on the circle around it. Set the angle with the dial, or drag the arrowhead along the circle.

d.Slide them together. The head of a goes to a crossing of the two circles and the tail of b goes to the same crossing. The ink cursor runs from the tail of a to the head of b. It is the turn for “a, then b”, the quaternion qbqa.

e.Reverse the order and the triangle swings to the other side of the crossing. The angle is the same and the axis is not: rotations do not commute. The far crossing gives the antipodal copy of the same triangle, and the same reading.

Plate II. One step, repeated, cuts its own scale: a ring for the point, and a ring for the turn.

a.Choose an axis by dragging its pole, and a step, a small rotation about it. The rotations by 0, 1, 2, … steps form a one-parameter subgroup, a straight line through the group.

b.Mark a point (drag the gold pin) and step it round. Each step cuts a division on the circle of latitude through it. The divisions are equal because every step is the same rotation, just as the log rule's scale is cut by multiplying by the same number again and again.

c.The turn of k steps is an arc of kφ/2 on the equator of the axis. When the point has come home, after 360°, the turn has gone only half way round: q = −1, the same rotation on the other sheet. It closes at 720°.

d.Choose a step that does not divide 360°, such as 7° or 25°, and the ring never quite closes: the point lands a little past where it began.

Plate III, a. The commutator a b a⁻¹ b⁻¹ of two small turns about perpendicular axes.

The belt

Plate III, b. A globe turned through 720°, and the ribbon that ties it to the frame.

a.Turn by b⁻¹, a⁻¹, b, a in that order (the commutator acts from the right), each through ε about the axes x and y. The pole z is carried round a small quadrilateral and comes home to within about ε³/√2.

b.The four turns together are not nothing. They are a rotation through about ε² radians about an axis near the pole (it leans from z by about ε/√2), shown by the witness on the equator. The quadrilateral encloses about ε² steradians, and the rotation goes round the pole the opposite way to the path. Both agree with ε² to second order, and drift apart as ε grows.

c.The turn of the globe grows by half the angle. At 360° the arc is a half circle and its ends are antipodal: q = −1. At 720° it is a whole circle and q = 1 again.

d.The ribbon is tied to the globe and to the frame. Its underside is hatched, so each crossing of the hatching is half a twist. At 360° it carries one full twist, and no motion of the ribbon alone will remove it. At 720° it carries two, and those can be removed, by passing the ribbon once round the globe while both ends stay put.

¶Commentary

Rotations and quaternions. A rotation of space about a unit axis n through an angle θ corresponds to the unit quaternion q = cos θ/2 + sin θ/2 (n₁i + n₂j + n₃k), and it acts on a vector v, written as a pure quaternion, by v ↦ q v q⁻¹. Composing rotations is multiplying quaternions: first q₁, then q₂, is q₂q₁. The unit quaternions form the group SU(2), a three-sphere, and q and −q give the same rotation. So SU(2) covers SO(3) twice.

Hamilton's arcs. Hamilton called a unit quaternion a versor, and in his Lectures on Quaternions (1853) drew it as an arc of a great circle on the unit sphere, an arc that may be slid along its own circle without changing the versor. For unit vectors A and B the quotient B A⁻¹ is the versor whose arc runs from A to B. Then B P⁻¹ · P A⁻¹ = B A⁻¹: arcs that meet head to tail at P compose to the arc from the first tail to the last head. That one line is the whole instrument.

Why half the angle. A reflection in the plane perpendicular to A, followed by a reflection in the plane perpendicular to B, is a rotation about A × B through twice the angle between A and B. So an arc of length θ/2 stands for a rotation through θ, and an arc of 180° stands for a full turn. Arcs longer than a half circle are allowed, and they are the other sheet of the cover: the arc of 360° − φ in one direction gives −q for the arc of φ in the other.

Rodrigues. Euler had already written a rotation with four parameters, the components of q, and Olinde Rodrigues in 1840 found how the parameters of two rotations combine to give those of the product. His rule is Hamilton's product, three years before quaternions. The same paper's formula v cos θ + (n × v) sin θ + n (n·v)(1 − cos θ), for turning one vector, is what draws every arc on these plates.

Order matters. The products qbqa and qaqb are conjugate, qaqb = qa(qbqa)qa⁻¹, so they turn through the same angle about different axes. On the rule this is the two triangles on either side of the crossing, mirror images in the plane through the two poles, with cursors of equal length on different circles.

Curvature. On a flat rule, sides add. On the sphere the triangle's third side falls short of the sum, and the shortfall is the curvature at work. For small turns ε about perpendicular axes the commutator a b a⁻¹ b⁻¹ is a rotation through ε² + O(ε³) about the third axis, the bracket [X, Y] = Z of the Lie algebra. The pole it carries round encloses an area that agrees to the same order, as the area of a small loop agrees with the turning of a vector carried round it, by Gauss and Bonnet.

Two sheets. A loop of rotations from the identity back to itself, once round about a fixed axis, cannot be shrunk to a point in SO(3); twice round, it can. The fundamental group π₁(SO(3)) is ℤ/2, and SU(2), being a three-sphere, is simply connected. The belt shows it with a ribbon instead of a loop: one twist is stuck, two can be undone. The demonstration is usually credited to Dirac, and the same sign change is the one an electron's wave function picks up under a single full turn.

Further. W. R. Hamilton, Lectures on Quaternions (1853); S. L. Altmann, Rotations, Quaternions, and Double Groups (1986), which uses the turns throughout; J. Stillwell, Naive Lie Theory (2008), for SU(2), SO(3), and the bracket.
