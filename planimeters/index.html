The Planimeter Bench

To know the area of a field on a map, you need not count squares. Run a point once round its edge, and a little wheel, a knife, or a pair of counters keeps an account that comes out to the area. Five such instruments are laid out here on one tracing table, each with its reading and its errors.

Plate I. The polar planimeter, after Amsler (1854): a pole, two arms, and a wheel.

a.The pole: a heavy weight with a needle beneath, pressed into the table. Drag it anywhere on the board.

b.The pole arm a and the tracer arm b, hinged at the elbow. The elbow can only swing on the dotted circle about the pole.

c.The measuring wheel rides on the tracer arm with its axle along the arm. It rolls when the arm moves sideways and skids when the arm slides along itself, so it counts only the sideways motion. Fig. 2 shows its drum, its vernier, and the disk that counts whole turns.

d.The tracer point, under a magnifying ring. Take it once round the curve and back to where it began. The area is the tracer arm's length times the distance the wheel rolled: A = b·w.

e.Drag the pole inside the curve. The elbow now goes all the way round its circle, and the wheel misses a fixed amount, π(a² + b² − 2bc), which is added to the reading.

Plate II. The linear planimeter: the elbow runs on a carriage along a straight rail.

a.The rail, a steel straightedge laid across the table. Drag it up or down.

b.The carriage runs on four rollers along the rail and carries the elbow, which therefore moves on a straight line instead of a circle.

c.The wheel on the tracer arm, the same as in Plate I. It rolls only with the arm's sideways motion.

d.The tracer point. Go once round, and A = b·w exactly, as before.

e.There is no correction term here. The elbow cannot go round anything, so the curve may be as long as the rail allows: a strip of a river, or a whole year of a recording chart.

Plate III. Prytz's hatchet (1875): a bent rod with a knife edge at one end and a point at the other.

a.The starting point S, near the middle of the curve. It begins at the centroid; drag it elsewhere and watch the error grow.

b.The knife edge. It can move along the rod but not across it, like the back wheel of a bicycle. Its track, dotted, is made of tractrices.

c.The point at the other end of the rod. Carry it from S straight out to the curve, once round, and straight back to S.

d.The rod comes home turned by a small angle θ. Then A ≈ L²θ, with θ in radians. The dashed line is where the rod began.

e.This is the one instrument here that is not exact. The leftover turn is a holonomy, and L²θ is only its first term; a longer rod and a start near the centroid make the rest small.

Plate IV. A radial planimeter: an arm turning about a centre, a tracer sliding on it, and a wheel on a glass disk.

a.The centre, and a disk of ground glass fixed to the table under it. Drag it; it may stand inside the curve or outside.

b.The arm turns about the centre, and the tracer slides along it in a slot. So the tracer's place is given by polar coordinates: r along the arm, θ round the disk.

c.A cam at the hub, worked by a cord from the slider, sets the wheel at the distance ρ = r²/2k from the centre. Its edge is cut to a parabola. (The cord and cam are drawn schematically.)

d.The wheel rests on the glass with its axle along the arm. When the arm turns by dθ it rolls ρ dθ = r² dθ/2k; when the wheel is moved in or out it only skids.

e.So the wheel totals (1/k)·½∮r² dθ, and the area is A = k·w. This is Green's theorem in polar form, and no correction is needed wherever the centre stands.

Plate V. The counting frame: a cross-slide whose two wheels count steps in x and in y.

a.Two fixed rails, above and below the sheet, and a bridge that runs along them. The bridge's place is x.

b.A carriage runs up and down the bridge and carries the tracer. Its place is y.

c.Two counting wheels, one on the bridge's foot and one on the carriage, click once for each step they roll. Fig. 2 shows their counters.

d.At each click of the y wheel, add the current x count times the step in y. The total is Σ x·Δy = ∮x dy, which is the area: Green's theorem done by counting.

e.Its only intrinsic error is the count itself: each reading is off by up to half a step. Make the step coarse and see how fast the error grows.

¶Commentary

Green's theorem. The area inside a closed curve can be read off the curve alone: A = ∮x dy = ½∮(x dy − y dx). Every instrument on this bench is a machine for one form of that integral. The tracer supplies the curve, and something that rolls, turns, or counts does the adding.

Why A = b·w. As the tracer arm moves, it sweeps area. If its elbow end moves sideways by dn and the arm turns by dφ, it sweeps b·dn + ½b²dφ. The wheel, at distance c from the elbow, rolls dw = dn + c·dφ. So the swept area is b·dw + b(½b − c)dφ. Round a closed curve the arm comes back to its old angle, so ∮dφ = 0, and the elbow, swinging back and forth on its arc, encloses nothing. What is left is A = b·w.

The pole inside. If the pole lies inside the curve, the elbow goes once round its circle of radius a and the arm turns once round, ∮dφ = 2π. The same bookkeeping gives A = b·w + πa² + 2πb(½b − c) = b·w + π(a² + b² − 2bc). The constant π(a² + b² − 2bc) is the area of the instrument's "zero circle", and it is usually given with the instrument.

The linear planimeter. Here the elbow runs on a straight line. It encloses no area, the arm returns to its angle, and A = b·w with no constant. Makers such as Coradi built them to run on a pair of heavy rollers instead of a rail, so that a long strip could be measured in one pass.

The hatchet. Prytz's rod is a different kind of machine. Its knife edge cannot move sideways, so the rod obeys a rule about direction rather than position, and a closed trip of the point need not bring the rod back to its old direction. The leftover angle is a holonomy, like the turn of a pendulum carried round the Earth. For a curve small compared with L, starting near its centroid, θ ≈ A/L², which is the rule A ≈ L²θ. The error falls as L grows, and grows as S moves off the centroid.

The radial planimeter. In polar coordinates about any centre, Green's theorem reads A = ½∮r² dθ. The instrument of Plate IV forms this by a disk-and-wheel integrator, of the kind James Thomson described and Kelvin used in his harmonic analyser, and Bush later in his differential analyser: a wheel resting on a disk at distance ρ from its centre rolls ρ dθ as the disk turns by dθ against it. A cam makes ρ = r²/2k, so the wheel's total is A/k. The design here is drawn to show the principle; I do not know of a maker who sold one in this form.

The counting frame. A cross-slide reads x and y separately, as a plotting table or a digitizer does. The area is then a sum, Σ x̄·Δy, taken step by step with the average x of each step. The only intrinsic error is the size of a count; the errors of the individual counts partly cancel round the curve.

Where error comes from. Tremor is the hand wandering off the line; it adds and removes slivers, which nearly cancel. Slip is the wheel skidding by a small random fraction at each step; for the hatchet it is the knife drifting sideways. Reading is the resolution of the dial: a vernier reads a thousandth of a turn, the dial alone a hundredth. Intrinsic is what the instrument gets wrong with a perfect hand: almost nothing for the exact instruments, and the whole L²θ approximation for the hatchet. The budget in each margin turns on one source at a time, with the same random draws.

Units. The sheet is 25 by 17.5 cm, ruled in centimetres. Every measuring wheel is 2 cm across, so one turn is 2π cm of rolling. The true area is computed from the curve by the shoelace formula.
