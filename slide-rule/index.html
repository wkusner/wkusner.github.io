The Slide Rule

Logarithms you can slide: a plain Mannheim rule, and the line it grew from.

A slide rule is a table of logarithms cut into wood. The distance from 1 to any number on its scales is the logarithm of that number, so laying one length after another adds logarithms, and adding logarithms multiplies numbers. Set the slide, move the hairline, and read three figures of the answer without a pencil.

Plate I. The C and D scales, the cursor, and the reading glass. The brackets above are the lengths being added.

Drag the slide or the cursor. Or focus either with Tab and use the arrow keys: each press goes to the next graduation of D, and Shift goes to the next figured one.

  1. Multiply 2 × 3. Set the left index of C over 2 on D, move the hairline to 3 on C, and read 6 on D.
  2. Multiply 2.4 × 3.5. Between 2 and 4 the marks go by 0.02, and every fifth one is longer, so 2.4 is the fourth longer mark past 2.
  3. Multiply 7 × 8. From the left index, 8 on C runs off the end of D. Use the right index, read 5.6, and put the point back by estimation: 7 × 8 is about 50, so 56.
  4. Divide 8.4 ÷ 2.4. Put the hairline on 8.4 on D, slide 2.4 on C under it, and read the quotient on D under the index.
  5. Chain 3 × 4 × 5 without writing down 12. Do 3 × 4 with the right index, leave the hairline on 1.2, bring the left index to the hairline, and go on to 5 on C.

a.The stock, mahogany faced with boxwood, carries the D scale. From 1 to 2 it is cut in hundredths, from 2 to 4 in fiftieths, and from 4 to 10 in twentieths: the scale crowds to the right, so the divisions get coarser and each stays about the same width.

b.The slide carries C, the same scale again. The 1s at its two ends are its indices. Setting an index over a on D adds the length log a to everything on C.

c.The cursor is a glass with a fine red hairline, held in brass. It carries a reading from one scale to the scale beside it.

d.The reading glass shows the scales under the hairline five times larger, so you can see which mark the hairline is on.

e.If b would fall past the right end, use the right index. The slide moves back one whole length, which is log 10, so D shows the figures of a × b ÷ 10. The rule gives figures, never the decimal point; you place the point by a rough estimate.

Plate II. Nine scales and one hairline. Every scale is read against D.

Drag the slide or the cursor, or use the arrow keys on either.

  1. Find √20. Put the hairline on 20 on A. Twenty has two figures before the point, so it lies in the right half of A; D reads 4.47. The left half would give √2.
  2. Find 3². Put the hairline on 3 on D; A reads 9.
  3. Find ∛50. Put the hairline on 50 on K, in the middle third; D reads 3.68.
  4. Find 1/7. Put the hairline on 7 on C; CI reads 1.43, which is 0.143.
  5. Find log 2. Put the hairline on 2 on D; L reads .301.

a.A and B fit the whole of D twice into the same length, so each is D at half scale: A over D is x² over x. The left half holds 1 to 10, the right half 10 to 100, and both are figured 1 to 9. For a square root, count the figures before the point: an odd count (2, 200) goes in the left half, an even count (20, 2000) in the right.

b.K fits D three times: cubes over D, and cube roots back. Count figures before the point in threes to choose the third.

c.CI, in red, is C run backwards: over b on C it shows 1/b. To multiply, divide by the reciprocal: set b on CI under the hairline at a on D, and a × b is on D under the index.

d.L is an ordinary even scale from 0 to 1. Over D it reads the mantissa of log₁₀: log 2 is .301 because 2 sits 0.301 of the way along D.

e.S and T are figured in degrees. The angle θ on S stands over 10 sin θ on D, and on T over 10 tan θ, so sin 30° is 5 on D, that is, 0.5. On many rules these two are on the back of the slide; here they sit on the stock.

Plate III. A line of numbers and a pair of dividers, after Gunter; then a second line laid against it, after Oughtred.

Drag either point to open the dividers, or the head to carry them. With the keyboard, each arrow press moves to the next graduation.

  1. Open the dividers from 1 to 3. They now hold the length log 3.
  2. Multiply 3 × 4. Keep that opening, carry the dividers, and set the left point on 4; the right point falls on 12.
  3. Solve 3 : 5 = 6 : x. Open the dividers from 3 to 5, carry them to 6, and read x = 10.
  4. Open the dividers from 1 to 2 and step them along four times. The points walk 2, 4, 8, 16, and 32 = 2⁵.
  5. Change to Oughtred's two lines, slide the upper 1 over 3, and read 12 below the upper 4. Every other product by 3 is there too.

a.The line of numbers is a single logarithmic line on a boxwood rule, running 1 to 10 and on to 100. The distance from 1 to any number is its logarithm, to scale.

b.The dividers are brass, with steel points that find the scratches of the graduation. Drag a point to open them; drag the head to carry them without changing the opening.

c.An opening of the dividers is a ratio. Laid from 1 to a, it is log a; laid again from b, it ends at a × b. Stepping it over and over gives a, a², a³, …, which is why equal steps on the line are equal ratios.

d.Oughtred's idea was to lay a second line against the first. Slide its 1 over a and every b on it stands over a × b: the dividers' one product becomes a whole table, and the slide rule is born.

¶Commentary

Logarithms. John Napier published his logarithms in 1614, in the Mirifici logarithmorum canonis descriptio, to turn the multiplications of astronomy into additions. Henry Briggs, after visiting Napier, recast them to base 10, so that multiplying by 10 adds exactly 1. He printed the logarithms of the first thousand numbers in 1617 and the great Arithmetica logarithmica in 1624. Base 10 is what lets a single cycle of the scale serve for every decade: 2, 20, and 0.002 share a mantissa and so share a mark.

Gunter's line. Edmund Gunter, professor of astronomy at Gresham College, had the idea around 1620 of drawing the logarithms instead of printing them: a line on a rule on which the distance from 1 to n is log n. With a pair of dividers the line multiplies, divides, and works proportions, as on Plate III. For two centuries Gunter's scale, with its lines of numbers, sines, and tangents, was a navigator's instrument.

Oughtred's slide. William Oughtred saw, around 1622, that two such lines held side by side and slid along each other do the work of the dividers, and do it for every number on the line at once. He described both a straight form and a circular one; his account was printed in 1632, in The Circles of Proportion, translated from his Latin by William Forster. Richard Delamain had published a circular rule in 1630, and the two men quarrelled bitterly over who was first.

Mannheim's rule. In the 1850s Amédée Mannheim, a French artillery officer, settled the arrangement most later rules kept: squares on A and B above, C and D below, all read together through a sliding cursor with a hairline. That is the rule of Plates I and II. Later patterns added cubes, reciprocals, logarithms, and trigonometric scales on the face and back, and the slide rule stayed in every engineer's pocket until the electronic calculator of the 1970s.

Why the scale is self-generating. A rule multiplies because the length from 1 to ab is the length from 1 to a plus the length from 1 to b; for a scale that increases steadily, this forces the length to be a fixed multiple of log. So the scale can be made from itself. The step from 1 to 2 also goes from 2 to 4 and from 4 to 8; the step from 1 to 3 goes from 3 to 9. Mark enough such steps and the rest fall between them. The stepping dividers of Plate III do exactly this.

Accuracy. On a rule 25 cm long, one division is about a millimetre or less, and a careful eye splits it into fifths. That gives about one part in a thousand: three significant figures, four near the left end where the scale is open. It is enough for engineering. The reckon beside each plate shows the exact value of what you set, so you can see the rule's error, which is the reader's error, since the scale itself is exact.

The decimal point. The rule knows only figures. The product 240 × 0.035 is set exactly as 2.4 × 3.5 and reads 840; a rough estimate, 200 × 0.04 = 8, puts the point: 8.40. Slide rule users learned to carry this estimate in their heads, and it is a habit worth having still.

Further. Florian Cajori, A History of the Logarithmic Slide Rule and Allied Instruments (1909), still the standard history of the early rules; Peter M. Hopp, Slide Rules: Their History, Models and Makers (1999), for the instruments themselves.
