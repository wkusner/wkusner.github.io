Napier's Bones

and the rods and boards that came after them.

In 1617, the year he died, John Napier of Merchiston published a small book on reckoning with rods, the Rabdologiae. Each rod carries one column of the times table. Lay the rods for a number side by side and any row of its multiplication table can be read across them, so only the sums along the diagonals are left to do. Nearly three centuries later, Genaille and Lucas cut rods that do those sums as well. In the same book Napier turned a chessboard into a calculator that works in binary.

Plate I. The bones, after Napier's Rabdologiae (1617): a board, the index rod, and a box of rods.

Click a rod in the box to lay it; click a rod on the board to put it back, or drag it (or use the arrow keys) to change its place.

  1. Multiply 425 × 6. Every diagonal sum is a single digit, so the row reads off directly.
  2. Multiply 425 × 8. One diagonal reaches 10: write 0 and carry 1 to the next.
  3. Multiply 3,071 × 8. The 0 rod is blank but keeps its place.
  4. Multiply 46,785,399 × 7: eight rods and nine strips, read in one pass.
  5. Multiply 425 × 367: three rows, each moved one place left, then added.

a.The box holds rods for the digits 0 to 9, as many of each as you need.

b.Each rod is one column of the times table. Its head shows a digit d, and cell n below holds n·d, with the tens above the slanting line and the units below it.

c.The index rod is fixed at the left of the board. Click one of its numbers to choose that row.

d.Read the chosen row across the rods. Each slanting strip holds the units of one product and the tens of the product to its right, and those belong to the same place. Add each strip, from right to left, and carry.

e.For a multiplier with several digits, read one row for each digit and add the rows, each moved one place further left, as in long multiplication. The rods give you the rows, and you still do that last sum on paper.

Plate II. The rulers of Henri Genaille and Édouard Lucas (1891). Follow the triangles from right to left, and the product reads off with no carrying.

Click a band on the index rod to choose the multiplier.

  1. Multiply 425 × 7 by following the triangles. Compare it with Plate I.
  2. Multiply 99,999 × 9. Every place carries 8, and you never write a carry down.
  3. Multiply 3,071 × 8, and watch which cell of each band you land in.
  4. Lay a number of six or more digits of your own and multiply it by 6.

a.Each rod has a band for each multiplier n from 2 to 9. The band has n cells, one for each carry that can come in from the right, 0 to n − 1. The cell for carry c shows the units digit of n·d + c.

b.Each black triangle has its broad end over the cells that pass on the same carry. Its point touches the rod to the left at the cell for that carry.

c.The index rod holds the multipliers, and beside each the digits 0 to n − 1. The last triangle points to one of them, and that is the first digit of the product.

d.Begin at the top cell of the band on the rightmost rod, since nothing is carried into the units. Read its digit and follow its triangle to the next rod, then read and follow again. The product comes out from right to left.

Plate III. Napier's chessboard (Rabdologiae, 1617). Each square is worth twice the square to its right and twice the square below it.

×

Click a square to put a counter on it, and click a counter to take it off. Drag a counter, or focus it and use the arrow keys, to move it.

  1. Lay 87 on the bottom row by hand, with one counter for each power of two in it.
  2. Add 13 and 11: lay both on the bottom row, then simplify.
  3. Double 13 by moving every counter one square to the left.
  4. Multiply 13 × 11 on the board.
  5. Multiply 25 × 19, and read the answer in Napier's letters.

a.Each square is worth a power of two: 1 at the bottom right, doubling with each step to the left and each step up. Its value is written in its corner. The number on the board is the sum of the squares that hold counters.

b.Napier named the values of the bottom row by letters, a = 1, b = 2, c = 4, d = 8, and so on, so 87 = 64 + 16 + 4 + 2 + 1 is written abceg.

c.To simplify, take two counters off one square and put one on the next square to the left, since 2·2k = 2k+1. Repeat until no square holds two, and the number is in binary.

d.One square down and one square left is worth the same, so a counter can slide down its slanting row to the bottom without changing the number.

e.To multiply, mark the multiplicand along the foot and the multiplier up the side. Put a counter wherever a marked column meets a marked row: it is worth 2i·2j = 2i+j. Slide them all down, and simplify.

¶Commentary

The lattice. The bones are a lattice multiplication cut into pieces. In the lattice, or gelosia, method you rule a grid with one column for each digit of the multiplicand and one row for each digit of the multiplier. You write each digit product in its cell, split by a diagonal into tens and units, and add along the diagonals. Italian arithmetic books of the fifteenth century, such as the Treviso Arithmetic of 1478, call it multiplying per gelosia, after the latticed screens set in Venetian windows. The method is older than that name, and appears earlier in Arabic arithmetic.

Napier's step. Each column of a lattice depends on only one digit, so it can be made once and kept. Napier carved each column of the times table on a rod. To multiply, you lay out rods for the multiplicand, and the multiplier digit picks the row. His own rods were square in section, with a column on each of the four faces, and the digits on opposite faces add to nine. The diagonal sums are never more than 9 + 8 + 1 = 18, so a carry is never more than 1.

The book. Rabdologiae, from the Greek for "rod" and "reckoning", appeared in Edinburgh in 1617, three years after Napier's tables of logarithms. Besides the rods for multiplication and division, it describes special rods that help extract square and cube roots. It also describes the promptuary, a box of strips that does whole multiplications without writing partial products, and, in an appendix, the location arithmetic of Plate III.

How the bones spread. Sets in wood, bone, and ivory were made all over Europe within a few years. Wilhelm Schickard's calculating machine of 1623 carried the bones on turning cylinders in its upper half and an adding mechanism below. Later makers mounted them on cylinders in boxes, as Gaspar Schott did in his Organum Mathematicum (1668). The bones also suggested a step to the slide rule: they show that a multiplication table can be built into a mechanism you read instead of memorize.

Genaille and Lucas. The bones leave the carrying to the user. Édouard Lucas asked whether rods could do it themselves, and Henri Genaille, a French engineer, answered with the rulers of Plate II, published in 1891. The key fact is that when you multiply by n, the carry into any place is at most n − 1, since 9n + (n − 1) is less than 10n. So each rod needs only n cells for each multiplier, one for each possible carry, and a triangle in each cell can show where its own carry sends you. Genaille also made rulers for division on the same plan.

Location arithmetic. On Napier's chessboard each square is worth a power of two, and a number is a set of counters, so this is arithmetic in base 2. Addition is putting counters together, and simplifying turns a heap into binary. Doubling is a shift one square left, and multiplying is the same placing and sliding as the lattice, done with powers of two instead of digits. Napier also did subtraction, division, and square roots on the board. It is binary arithmetic nearly ninety years before Leibniz wrote about the binary numerals in 1703, though Napier treated it as a device for reckoning, not as a way of writing numbers.
