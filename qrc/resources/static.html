Short guides

Your first visit to the QRC

QRC guide · 3 min read

Drop-in tutoring is free. You don't need an appointment, and you don't need to have finished the assignment.

Before you come. Bring the problem, your notes, and the textbook or Canvas page it came from. If you can, try the problem first and mark where you got stuck. A half-finished page is fine. So is a blank one.

When you get there. Check the tutor board by the door to see who's on. Sign in, then tell the tutor your course and what you want to be able to do by the time you leave. Tutors often work with a few people at once, so you may start on your own for a few minutes.

What tutors do. They ask questions, work a similar example with you, and check your reasoning. They won't do graded work for you, and they follow your course's collaboration policy.

If drop-in isn't the right fit. To get a tutor for a specific course, request one through Course tutors through Navigate. For time management or study habits, meet with a coach at the Center for Academic Success.

Stuck on a problem? A 10-minute routine

QRC guide · 2 min read

Most "I don't get any of it" turns out to be one stuck step. This routine finds it.

The routine

  1. Write what the problem asks for in your own words.
  2. List what you know: the given values, plus the definitions and formulas from this section.
  3. Find the exact step where you stall. Is it a definition, choosing a method, the algebra, or reading the problem?
  4. Find a worked example of that step in your textbook or in Paul's Online Math Notes. Copy it out by hand, then try yours again.
  5. Check your answer: units, sign, a rough estimate, or a quick graph in Desmos graphing calculator.
  6. Still stuck after 10 minutes? Bring what you wrote to the QRC. Your notes from steps 1–3 make a tutor's job much faster.

Studying for a math exam in 10 days

QRC guide · 4 min read

Cramming feels productive and isn't. Short, mixed practice spread over a week and a half works better.

10 days out. List every topic on the exam from the syllabus or your instructor. Collect two or three practice problems per topic from homework, textbook review exercises, or old exams if your instructor shares them.

Days 9 to 4. Do 30–40 minutes a day of mixed problems, without looking at your notes first. Mixing topics feels harder, and that's why it works (Six strategies for effective learning). Keep an error log: for each miss, write what went wrong and the fix.

Days 3 and 2. Take a timed practice exam. Spend the next day on your error log only, and bring the two hardest problems to the QRC.

The day before. Lightly review your error log and formulas, and read Common math errors. Sleep matters more than one more hour of problems.

Tip. To build a weekly study plan, meet with a coach at the Center for Academic Success.

Getting ready for the ALEKS placement

QRC guide · 3 min read

ALEKS decides where you start in Lawrence math: MATH 102, MATH 103, or calculus (MATH 140).

How it works. ALEKS adapts to your answers, so it measures what you know rather than how many questions you get right. Scores of 75 or more place into MATH 140, 45–74 into MATH 103, and below 45 into MATH 102 (ALEKS math placement).

Take it straight. Use only the tools the test gives you. A placement that's too high makes the course harder, not easier.

Check yourself first. The QRC self-check for MATH 102 or 103 shows which skills to review, with a worksheet for each.

If you want to place higher. After your first attempt, ALEKS gives you learning modules matched to your gaps. To retake the proctored test, wait 48 hours and spend at least five hours in the modules. Khan Academy and Paul's Online Notes: Algebra are good companions.

Not sure which course is right?. Talk with your adviser, or stop by the QRC during drop-in hours.

How to learn from a free textbook

QRC guide · 3 min read

Reading math isn't like reading a novel. Read with a pencil, and expect to go slowly.

The routine

  1. Skim the section headings and the boxed definitions first, so you know where you're going.
  2. For each worked example, cover the solution and try it yourself before reading it.
  3. Do the short practice problems right after each example. In OpenStax books these are labeled "Try It," and answers are in the back.
  4. If your course uses a different book, match by topic, not chapter number. Use the book's search or index.

Tip. Many Lawrence math courses use OpenStax books, so your course text may already be free (OpenStax textbook library). For extra practice with full solutions, the CLP Calculus texts and problem books problem books are hard to beat.

Getting the most out of math videos

QRC guide · 2 min read

Watching someone else solve a problem feels like learning. It only counts once you can do one yourself.

The routine

  1. Watch with paper and pencil, not in bed.
  2. Pause before each step and predict what comes next.
  3. Speed up the parts you know and slow down the parts you don't.
  4. Right after, do one problem on your own with the video closed.
  5. If three videos haven't helped, switch to a person. Come to the QRC.

Tip. Good places to start: Khan Academy for short skill videos, Professor Leonard lectures for full lectures, Essence of Calculus (3Blue1Brown) for the big ideas, and StatQuest for statistics.

Asking your professor for help

QRC guide · 2 min read

Office hours are for every student, not only for students in trouble. Professors like specific questions.

In person. Go to office hours with one or two specific problems and the work you've tried. It's fine to say, "I don't know where to start."

By email. Put the course and topic in the subject line. Say what you tried, ask one clear question, and attach a photo of your work.

Subject: MATH 140, question on 3.4 #12

Hi Professor ___,

I'm working on problem 12 in section 3.4. I used the chain rule (my work is attached), but my answer doesn't match the back of the book. Is my setup right, or could I come to office hours Tuesday to go over it?

Thanks,
[Your name]

Your first shift as a QRC tutor

QRC guide · 3 min read

You don't need to know everything. You need a routine and the confidence to say, "Let's find out together."

Open (three minutes). Ask, "What's your goal today?" and "Show me where it stalled." Agree on at most two things to get done.

Work. Ask for decisions ("What tells you to use substitution here?"). Model one step, do the next together, then let them do one alone. When several students are waiting, give each a next step and rotate.

Close (three minutes). Have the student summarize what they learned in a sentence or two, and suggest when to practice again. Then write the session summary in Navigate.

When you don't know. Say so, and look it up together in Paul's Online Math Notes or the course text. Students learn a lot from watching how you get unstuck.

Tip. The QRC trains to the CRLA tutor training certification (ITTPC) standards. The scenarios in QMaSC Handbook: training and mentoring chapters make good practice.

Which statistics, data science, or CS course comes first?

QRC guide · 3 min read

A map of the course sequences in statistics, data science, and computer science, from the 2026–27 catalog. Check the official course descriptions before you register.

Statistics

  1. Start with STAT 107 (no prerequisite). If you have credit for MATH 140, BIOL 170, or AP Statistics, start with STAT 255 instead.
  2. STAT 255, Statistical Modeling, leads to STAT 455, Advanced Statistical Modeling.
  3. The theory path: MATH 155, then MATH 200 and MATH 230, then MATH/STAT 340 (Probability), then STAT 445 (Mathematical Statistics) or STAT 450 (Bayesian Statistics).
  4. Resources for each course: statistics.

Data science

  1. Start with DASC 110, Data Science I (or ANTH 207 or BIOL 280). Then DASC 210, Data Science II.
  2. DASC 420, Advanced Predictive Modeling, needs STAT 255 and MATH 205 or MATH 250.
  3. STAT/CMSC 208, Statistical Machine Learning, needs one of CMSC 140, CMSC 150, CMSC 205, or DASC 110.
  4. The major also needs MATH 140 and a linear algebra course: MATH 205 (Applied Linear Algebra, after MATH 140) or MATH 250.
  5. Resources for each course: data science and applied linear algebra.

Computer science

  1. Majors start with CMSC 150 (Java). CMSC 140 (Python) is designed for non-majors and also opens CMSC 225 and STAT 208.
  2. CMSC 250 needs a C− or better in CMSC 150, plus an ALEKS score of 75 or a C− or better in MATH 103.
  3. CMSC 270 (Data Structures, in C++) needs a C− or better in CMSC 250. Most 400-level courses need CMSC 270.
  4. CMSC 510 and 515 also need MATH 230. The major needs MATH 140, 155, and 230.
  5. Resources for each course: computer science.

If the math is the problem. CMSC 250 and MATH 205 both lean on the ALEKS 75 level. The QRC self-check shows which skills to work on for MATH 102, 103, 140, and 155.

Tip. Course numbers and prerequisites change. The data science and statistics and computer science course descriptions are the official word.
